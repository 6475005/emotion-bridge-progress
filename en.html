1Summary

Aim
Transfer body motion learned from recordings of human speech and gesture to the tabletop robot Sota, and test whether it raises the interlocutor's sense of affinity and empathy (feeling heard and understood) in short emotional everyday conversation.
Status
A cascade is implemented in software: frozen Qwen3-Omni produces the reply text and speech, and a body-motion model conditioned on that speech generates 30 Hz joint rotations. Synchronized speech-and-motion playback was verified in a browser on four actual turns (2 Japanese, 2 English), 2.1–3.0 s from submission to playback start.
Key evidence
On development data the error is about 14% lower than generation without audio, and rises about 10% when the audio is swapped for another recording, so the motion does respond to the reply speech. It remains worse than a static mean pose on pointwise error, with repetitive poses and little movement (Section 6).
Not yet done
Physical execution on Sota, speech–motor synchronization, checks with a physical microphone and camera, and participant evaluation. Robot-side work is stopped while waiting for the unit's SDK and connection details.
vs. first paper
The first paper (ICIC 2024) authored bodily expression from LLM emotion scores and hand-designed motion rules. Here the motion itself is learned from human recordings.

2Research question and positioning

Research question: When body motion learned from recordings of human speech and gesture is presented on Sota in sync with the dialogue model's reply speech, does the interlocutor feel more heard and understood (affinity, empathy) in a short emotional everyday conversation?

  • H1（motion presence）：With identical reply speech, learned motion scores higher than an initial-pose hold on motion fit, felt understanding, and willingness to continue.
  • H2（learned vs. authored）：Learned motion is at least as good as a re-implemented first-paper baseline (authored, Laban-inspired rule motion). Superiority is a hypothesis, not a result.

The candidate primary outcome is the participant's own rating of feeling understood by the robot; naturalness, humanlikeness, and response appropriateness are secondary. Items, scales, and sample size are fixed before the main evaluation (Section 5).

Intended contribution: (a) a reproducible route from a frozen dialogue/speech model's reply speech to learned body motion mapped onto an 8-DoF tabletop robot, (b) physical operation in short emotional conversations in Japanese (secondarily English), and (c) participant evidence that separates the effect of adding motion from the effect of the learned method.

Relation to closest work

Motion-Omni (arXiv:2609.04250) generates speech and full-body motion jointly in one model, and a HAI Symposium 2026 report adds motion tokens to a Japanese full-duplex dialogue model. This system is instead a cascade that feeds completed reply speech into a separate model, with the output meant for a low-DoF physical robot. "Integration" means different things in the two families; differences and any advantage must be shown by implementation comparison and evaluation.

Where the laboratory's first paper and empathetic-dialogue systems on Pepper (ICSR+AI 2025; Lee et al., 2026) use authored or LLM-scripted gesture repertoires, the motion here is learned from human recordings. Compared with learned speech-driven gesture on humanoids (e.g. Yoon et al., 2019, NAO), this work differs in (1) conditioning on the dialogue model's own reply speech, (2) mapping to an 8-DoF tabletop robot, and (3) measuring felt understanding in Japanese emotional everyday conversation. Using Sota or Japanese is not by itself the contribution.

4System overview and mapping to Sota

Dialogue and speech use Qwen3-Omni as released (a public NVFP4-quantized derivative of the 30B-class MoE model) with frozen weights. The body-motion model is a separately trained diffusion model (about 14.06 M trainable parameters) conditioned only on features of the reply speech; the reply text is not fed to it. There is no simultaneous generation and no joint training of the two models.

The language (Japanese/English) is chosen by the user in the UI; the choice only switches the body model's output route (Japanese rig, 120 dims / English Trinity rig, 150 dims). Qwen's reply language follows the system prompt "reply in the same language as the user"; there is no automatic detection.

The runtime is one RTX 5090 (32 GB). Qwen takes about 98% of GPU memory and the body model lives in the remaining ~0.4 GB inside the same process. The container has no network access and calls no external API. Readiness takes about 273 s after launch, and a session is limited to one hour and 100 requests.

Mapping to Sota and what is lost

Sota has 8 DoF (torso yaw 1, each arm 2, head 3). The model outputs rotations for 20–25 joints, so the mapping keeps upper-arm direction and head pose in 8 coordinates and drops elbow flexion, wrists, fingers, torso tilt, and facial expression (bent forearms in the English data are lost, as verified). The head is zeroed at each reply's first generated frame. The mapping is geometric; servo signs, offsets, ranges, and speed limits are decided once unit information is available.

The first thing to check in the rehearsal is whether the mapped motion remains distinguishable to a viewer. If not, the mapping and channel allocation are revised before any further training.

Three stages. (1) Development rehearsal operated by me, recording expression, synchronization, and failures on the unit. (2) Lab pilot: matched same-speech pairs (A vs. B) viewed in counterbalanced order, to fix items and procedure. The earlier "20 members" was a development proposal, not a power calculation. (3) Main study: the system is frozen and independent participants converse with Sota. Primary outcome, contrast, and sample size are fixed beforehand, and pilot participants and stimuli are excluded.

Draft items (5-point): the robot's movements fit its reply / I felt understood by the robot / it was comfortable to talk with / I would like to talk with it again; plus a paired preference (with "no preference") and free text. Alignment with existing scales (Godspeed, perceived-empathy scales) is examined in the pilot.

Scenario: about two minutes, roughly six exchanges (provisional), everyday topics with mild emotion (discouragement after a presentation, joy at finishing a task, plans for tomorrow). Evaluation is primarily in Japanese; English is used to check generality of the motion generation. No clinical or counselling effect is claimed.

Records and ethics: All replies, speech, motion, and failures are logged; analysis accounts for repeated observations within participants and scenarios. Before any participant study I will consult on whether an ethics application is required and apply, with consent forms, recording policy, and freedom to withdraw. LLM replies use a fixed prompt and are screened beforehand.

6.2 Generation error over training, and how to read it

From 4,000 to 16,000 updates (control) the error fell by about 10% (Japanese) and 14% (ZEGGS). Adding the English (Trinity) route in the provisional v5 raised the error by +0.83% (Japanese) and +1.40% (ZEGGS); bilingual output was prioritized because only this version generates English reply motion. Each condition is a single run; seed variance is unmeasured.

On pointwise error the static mean pose beats every model. That is the price of generating motion that departs from the mean, not evidence that standing still is better. The meaningful difference is with-audio versus audio-free (about 14% lower error in Japanese), which shows the model responds to the speech it is given. Velocity-direction agreement, however, is near zero: the timing of the recorded motion is not reproduced. A diagnostic on the earlier Japanese model likewise found that the correct audio lowers error by 9.9% relative to substituted audio and improves velocity direction in 87 of 102 recordings.

6.3 Known failures (provisional model)

  • On actual replies the poses converge on near-hip arms and upright stances; congratulatory, empathetic, and questioning replies look alike.
  • Under-animation: generated variance is about 55% of the recorded variance (Trinity development, control).
  • Timing mismatch: velocity-direction cosine ≤ 0.06.
  • Occasional jumps at 4-second window seams (to be absorbed by interpolation and velocity limits on the robot).
  • Training used human recordings while deployment uses Qwen's synthetic voice (speaker "aiden"); the effect of this shift is unmeasured.

7Shortening the gap before the reply (changes and re-measurement)

With the previous implementation (the whole reply generated before anything is returned) the gap from utterance end to output grew with reply length; the median over 16 recorded-speech turns was 1.91 s, too long for a conversation study. Five changes were implemented and the gap re-measured.

  1. Streamed reply speech：the asynchronous engine returns speech in chunks of about two seconds, forwarded to the client as they arrive. The first body window is generated once a set amount of reply speech exists (1 s at measurement time; 2 s after the development-data check below), the rest of the window zero-padded; later windows every four seconds. Models, weights and sampling are unchanged.
  2. Relay removed：the service port is published directly; the SSH / docker-exec relay is gone.
  3. Fewer diffusion steps：body generation uses 16 instead of 50 steps. In isolation a window takes about 0.05 s instead of 0.16 s, but for the first window, which shares the GPU with the running speech stages, the median stayed at about 0.15 s (contention).
  4. Faster end-of-speech detection：a voice-activity detector with 20 ms frames and a 250 ms hangover sends the request as soon as it fires (push-to-talk: 0 s).
  5. Reply-length policy：a "one short sentence" prompt with an 80-token limit did not shorten replies reliably and changes the content, so it is not adopted.

8Current limits and risks

  • Physical execution on Sota has not been verified; software playback does not guarantee robot execution.
  • No participant evaluation has been run; the improvement in affinity and empathy is a hypothesis.
  • Speech and motion come from separate models (no joint training). The gap before the reply was measured on the server side and in a browser with synthetic devices; the value in a live conversation with a physical microphone, loudspeaker and robot is not yet measured.
  • Motion quality targets sufficiency for a short experiment, not human-level fidelity, and even that is not yet confirmed on the robot.
  • Microphone and camera input exist as a browser client checked with synthetic devices (a recorded utterance and a test pattern); a physical microphone and camera in a room have not been tried.

10Glossary

Body-motion model
Diffusion model that generates 30 Hz joint rotations conditioned on the reply speech. Internal name full-v5; the 16,000-update weights are provisionally adopted.
Qwen3-Omni
Public foundation model that understands audio/images, writes the reply, and synthesizes speech. Its weights are not updated here.
Cascade
Motion is generated after the reply speech is complete, as opposed to streaming generation during speech synthesis.
4-second window
Unit of training and generation: 120 frames at 30 Hz. Reply speech is cut into 4-second pieces, the tail zero-padded.
Generated history
The last 30 generated frames of the previous window condition the next window; reset between replies.
6D rotation, MSE
Joint rotations in a 6-value form (Japanese 20 joints = 120 dims, Trinity 25 joints = 150 dims). MSE is the mean squared error against recorded motion; not a naturalness score.
Development data
Windows from recordings not used for training. Consulted repeatedly during development, so not an untouched test set.
own-audio / audio-free
Generation with the reply (or recorded) speech as condition / without audio. The difference shows how much the model responds to speech.
Schematic display
Coordinates for Sota's 8 DoF played back as a browser diagram; not servo commands.
Initial-pose hold
Control with the same speech and start pose but no motion; estimates the effect of adding motion.
Authored baseline
A re-implemented first-paper method (LLM emotion scores + Laban-inspired rule motion) used as the moving comparator.
ZEGGS / Trinity II / BEAT2 / GENEA 2023
Public human speech–motion datasets. ZEGGS and Trinity II are used for training, BEAT2 for pretraining, GENEA 2023 only in separate prototypes.

App.Appendices (collapsible)

AModel architecture and runtime details
  • Breakdown of the 14,055,926 trainable parameters: a shared trunk copied from the BEAT2-English parent and fine-tuned, 13,692,416 (≈97%); Japanese route 171,552; ZEGGS route 53,280; ZEGGS styles 4,608; Trinity route 134,070. The frozen parent (13,757,822) and codecs (Japanese 708,504, Trinity 731,574) are excluded.
  • Speech features: the reply speech is resampled to 16 kHz and cut into non-overlapping 4-second (64,000-sample) windows, tail zero-padded. The Qwen3-Omni Thinker audio encoder (≈650 M parameters, bf16) is called in the same process, giving 52×2048 features per window (bf16→float32), normalized with statistics computed from the fit split only.
  • Two history paths: the last 30 decoded frames of the previous window enter as 30 history tokens, and the first 30 of 150 latent slots (context) are denoised jointly with the 120 current slots. The Transformer processes 180 tokens (181 for Trinity, with a pose token).
  • For Trinity, "pose" is the window-mean pose (25 joints × 6D = 150 dims) and "dynamics" the 32-dim latent of the mean-removed residual. Japanese and ZEGGS predict the latent only.
  • Sampling: 50 timesteps of a 1,000-step cosine schedule, v-prediction DDIM (η = 0), no guidance. The initial noise is seeded from the reply ID, and FP32 with TF32 disabled makes results reproducible for the same reply and features.
  • The 18 ZEGGS style embeddings are used only in training; the live Japanese/English routes pass no style. Emotion style is not controlled.
  • Fixed dialogue conditions: system prompt "You are a conversational partner. Reply in the same language as the user, briefly in one or two sentences. Respond to their feelings without inventing personal experiences.". Thinker temperature 0.2, max 160 tokens; Talker temperature 0.9, top-k 50; speaker aiden; seed 17. History ≤ 3 turn pairs, context 2,048 tokens.
  • The Trinity 8,000→16,000 continuation took 511 s of training (586 s including loading and generation), peak CUDA 9.83 GB, on one RTX 5090; it can be re-run in minutes if needed.

DLatency measurement details

Gap from utterance end to reply output (measured inside the server)

On 2026-09-15, a client inside the same container as the resident service sent real recorded speech (8 Japanese, 8 English) and 4 text inputs one turn each. t0 = the moment the user's speech ends and the whole recording is available (for text, submission); t2 = the moment both the reply speech and the body motion are decoded and could start playing. Because the reply speech is returned complete, speech and motion start together.

EReferences, data, and rights

Groups: [prior] work compared or positioned against, [used] models and software actually used, [data] public datasets used for training and validation, [background] foundational references for representations and methods. Listing a related method does not mean its code or weights were adopted. Third-party models, data, and papers remain under their owners' terms and are not redistributed here.

  1. priorZhong Qiang, Yukiharu Nagai, Hisato Fukuda, Ryota Suzuki, Yoshinori Kobayashi. Impression Evaluation of Chat Robot with Bodily Emotional Expression Incorporating Large-Scale Language Models. ICIC 2024, LNCS 14871, pp. 439–449.
    The author's first paper (Kobayashi Laboratory): authored bodily expression and impression evaluation; basis of this study's authored baseline. Its results are not outcomes of this study.
  2. priorMotion-Omni. arXiv:2609.04250; author implementation (step-out/Motion-Omni).
    Closest joint generation of reply speech and full-body avatar motion in one model; differs from this cascade. Trained weights and evaluation code were not yet released as of September 2026.
  3. priorTowards Real-time Simultaneous Generation of Speech and Body Movements through Extension of Japanese Full-Duplex Dialogue Models. HAI Symposium 2026, P2-34.
    Simultaneous speech and motion by adding motion tokens to a Japanese full-duplex model (J-Moshi). Simultaneous generation is not implemented here.
  4. priorMultimodal Dialogue for Empathetic Human-Robot Interaction. ICSR+AI 2025 (Heriot-Watt University).
    Empathetic dialogue system on Pepper; a system-study precedent, not evidence of this study's novelty.
  5. priorLee et al. Generating Natural and Expressive Robot Gestures through Iterative Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback using LLMs. arXiv:2606.18747 (2026).
    LLM-scripted Pepper gestures refined with human feedback; fixed phrases and an authored repertoire, unlike learned continuous motion.
  6. priorKucherenko et al. The GENEA Challenge 2023: A large-scale evaluation of gesture generation models in monadic and dyadic settings. arXiv:2308.12646.
    Evaluation design reference (humanlikeness, speech fit, interlocutor fit); its data were used only in separate prototypes.
  7. usedQwen3-Omni Technical Report. arXiv:2509.17765.
    Foundation model for dialogue, audio understanding, and speech; weights not updated.
  8. usedYihongJin/Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct-NVFP4-W4A4-full-thinker-awqclip (Hugging Face).
    The quantized release actually deployed.
  9. usedvLLM-Omni.
    Runtime for resident multimodal inference.
  10. usedSota（ヴイストン株式会社）製品情報.
    Product specification (8 DoF); not a substitute for the laboratory unit's configuration and calibration.
  11. dataKenta Takeuchi, Souichirou Kubota, Keisuke Suzuki, Dai Hasegawa, Hiroshi Sakuta. Creating a gesture-speech dataset for speech-based automatic gesture generation. HCI International 2017, Springer, pp. 198–202.
    Original recording of the Japanese speech–gesture data (interview setting, one performer's motion, 1,047 utterances); the source of the Japanese archive used here.
  12. dataKucherenko et al. Analyzing Input and Output Representations for Speech-Driven Gesture Generation (IVA 2019); Moving Fast and Slow (IJHCI 2021); author code and linked archive.
    Representation-learning work on the dataset above; the archive (WAV/BVH) was obtained through the link in the released implementation.
  13. dataGhorbani et al. ZeroEGGS: Zero-shot Example-based Gesture Generation from Speech. arXiv:2209.07556; Ubisoft La Forge dataset.
    English speech–motion data (one actor, 19 styles), used for training; non-commercial research, not redistributed.
  14. dataTrinity Speech-Gesture Dataset II (Ferstl et al., ExpressGesture, 2021).
    English speech–motion data (one speaker); a selected subset trains the English reply route.
  15. dataLiu et al. EMAGE / BEAT2. CVPR 2024; arXiv:2401.00374.
    Used for English body pretraining; not a reproduction of EMAGE.

  17. backgroundSong et al. Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models. arXiv:2010.02502.
